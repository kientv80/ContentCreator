## Post 1: The Toothbrush Balance Trick

Dear old friends,

Last spring I reached for a watering can on the bottom step of the porch, turned a little too quickly, and found myself sitting in the begonias. Nothing broken except my pride and one very flat begonia. But my granddaughter, who happened to see the whole performance, asked me a question that stuck: "Grandpa, when did you last practice standing on one leg?"

I laughed. Then I realized the honest answer was "sometime around 1968."

Balance, I've learned, is a bit like a language. If you don't use it, you lose the words. The good news is that most of us can win a good deal of it back with small, steady practice, and you don't need a gym membership or a pair of shiny sneakers. You need a kitchen counter and about two minutes.

Here is what I do now, and what my physical therapist friend down the street approved:

- **The toothbrush trick:** While brushing my teeth, I stand near the sink and lift one foot an inch off the floor. One minute on the left, one minute on the right. The sink is right there to grab if I wobble, and I wobble plenty.
- **Heel-to-toe walk:** Along the hallway, I walk as if on a tightrope, heel touching toe, one hand trailing the wall.
- **Sit-to-stand:** Five times, I stand up from a sturdy chair without using my hands, then sit back down slowly. Slowly is the secret ingredient.
- **Ankle circles:** While the kettle boils, I draw little circles with each foot. Ankles are the unsung heroes of staying upright.
- **Clear the path:** I picked up the loose rug in the hallway and added a nightlight. The best fall is the one that never had a chance.

A word of caution, old friends: start gently, always have something solid nearby to hold, and if you feel dizzy, have fallen before, or notice your balance getting worse, talk with your doctor. Dizziness can have many causes, and a good checkup is the smartest first step. Many communities also offer gentle balance classes, and tai chi groups are wonderfully welcoming to beginners with creaky knees.

It took me about six weeks before I could stand on my left foot for a full minute without grabbing the sink. My right foot is still the show-off of the pair. But last week I stepped down from the porch with a full watering can, turned to wave at the mail carrier, and stayed perfectly upright. The begonias, I believe, were relieved.

What I love most is that this practice is a small daily promise to my future self. Every wobbly minute at the sink is a deposit in the bank of staying independent, of walking to the garden, of chasing grandchildren (slowly) across the lawn.

> *One foot up, the other steady,*
> *Brush and wobble, wobble, ready.*
> *Small daily steps, a stronger me,*
> *Still standing tall, like an old oak tree.*

Wishing you steady feet, sturdy ankles, and plenty of reasons to get up and go.

*Elder Friend AI*

## Post 2: Letters by Hand, Friendships Kept Warm

Dear old friends,

Last month I found a shoebox in the back of the closet. Inside were letters from my old friend Walter, written over forty years in his terrible handwriting. Letters from the Army, letters from his honeymoon, a letter that simply said, "My tomatoes failed. Send advice and sympathy." I sat on the bedroom floor reading them until my knees complained louder than I did.

Walter and I still talk on the phone, but it struck me that we hadn't written a real letter to each other in nearly twenty years. So that very afternoon I took out a pen and wrote him two pages about my fig tree, my bad knee, and a joke I'd heard at the hardware store. Ten days later a fat envelope arrived. He had written four pages. Walter has always had to win.

There is something about a handwritten letter that a quick message can't quite match. You can hold it. You can read it again on a gray afternoon. You can tell from the slant of the words whether your friend was in a hurry or settled in with a cup of coffee.

Staying connected matters for us older folks. Friendship keeps the mind busy, gives us something to look forward to, and reminds us we still matter to someone. Here are a few ways I've been keeping the letters going:

- **Keep a letter kit:** A box with notecards, envelopes, stamps, and a pen that writes smoothly. If it's all in one place, there's no excuse.
- **Pick a regular day:** I write on Sunday afternoons. One letter a week feels just right.
- **Write small things:** Nobody needs a novel. What you ate, what bloomed, what made you laugh. The small things are the friendship.
- **Ask a question:** End with something your friend can answer. It keeps the conversation rolling back and forth.
- **Include a little extra:** A pressed leaf, a newspaper cartoon, a photo of the garden. Walter sent me a seed packet last time.
- **Use big, comfortable handwriting:** If your hand gets tired, write shorter letters more often. A sore hand that lasts should be mentioned to your doctor.

Writing by hand is also good exercise for the mind. You have to remember, organize your thoughts, and spell (more or less). I've noticed my memory for stories sharpens after an hour of writing.

If you don't have a Walter, think of a cousin, a former neighbor, a grandchild away at school, or someone at your church or community center who lives alone. A letter arriving in an empty mailbox can light up someone's whole week.

> *Ink on paper, slow and true,*
> *Carries my old heart to you.*
> *Miles apart, but not alone,*
> *A letter feels like coming home.*

Wishing you full mailboxes, faithful friends, and a good pen that never runs dry.

*Elder Friend AI*
